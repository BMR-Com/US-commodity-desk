
Crop Production 

ISSN: 1936-3737

Released October 9, 2026, by the National Agricultural Statistics Service 
(NASS), Agricultural Statistics Board, United States Department of 
Agriculture (USDA).

Corn production for grain is forecast at 16.0 billion bushels, up 1 percent from the previous forecast but down 5 percent from 2025.
If realized, this would be the second highest grain production on record for the Nation. The average yield is forecast at 181.2 
bushels per acre, up 2.7 bushels from the previous forecast and but down 5.3 bushels from 2025.

Soybean production for beans is forecast at a record high 4.56 billion bushels, up 1 percent from the previous forecast and up 7 percent
from 2025. Yields are expected to average a record high 53.1 bushels per acre, up 0.3 bushel from the previous forecast and up 0.1 bushel
from 2025.

All cotton production is forecast at 13.4 million 480-pound bales, up 2 percent from the previous forecast but down 3 percent from 2025. 
The average yield is forecast at 790 pounds per harvested acre, up 14 pounds from the previous forecast but down 62 pounds from 2025.

This report was approved on October 9, 2026.

September Weather Summary

Highlights: El Ni o-driven changes began to significantly influence 
North American weather patterns during September, leading to multiple rounds 
of heavy rain from the Southwest into the Midwest, as atmospheric moisture 
from a swarm of Pacific tropical cyclones was drawn northeastward. Eastern 
Pacific cyclones of note during September included Hurricanes Marie, Odalys, 
and Polo. Among those hurricanes, only Polo made landfall in Mexico, striking 
Baja California Sur late September 28 and crossing the Gulf of California 
into southern Sonora early the next day. Less than a week earlier, on 
September 22, Polo had become the second-strongest tropical cyclone on 
record-based on a minimum central pressure of 892 millibars (26.34 inches of 
mercury)-for areas of the Pacific Ocean north of the equator and east of the 
International Dateline.

As surges of tropical moisture wrapped around the western and northern edges 
of a stubborn ridge of high pressure and interacted with a variety of 
disturbances and cold fronts, September rainfall totaled at least twice 
normal from portions of the Four Corners States into the western Corn Belt. 
More than a foot of rain fell in some Midwestern locations, including 
Des Moines, Iowa, where the monthly sum of 14.50 inches represented its 
second-wettest September on record, behind only 14.81 inches in 1914. For 
some locations, including Lincoln, Nebraska, it was the wettest September on 
record, with 11.49 inches (previously, 8.32 inches in 1914). The heavy rain, 
spread across several weeks, curtailed fieldwork, including corn and soybean 
harvesting, and threatened crop quality.

Related to El Ni o's disruptive influence on tropical cyclone intensification 
over the Atlantic Ocean, a satellite-era record was established for the 
latest date of the season's first Atlantic Basin hurricane. Prior to this 
year, the latest initial Atlantic hurricane formation had occurred on 
September 11, 2002, and 2013. Through September, there were no Atlantic Basin 
hurricanes and eight tropical storms, three of which made landfall on the 
Gulf Coast of the United States-most recently Edouard near Johnson Bayou, 
Louisiana, on September 1. Edouard was a small tropical storm that moved 
inland with sustained winds near 60 mph and had a relatively small footprint 
of heavy rain and gusty winds, mainly across eastern Texas.

Showery weather helped to limit September wildfire activity, except in parts 
of California and the Great Basin. Even with fewer explosive fires, the 
Nation's year-to-date wildfire burned area topped 9 million acres by early 
October, nearly 150 percent of the 10-year average of 6.1 million acres. 
Impressively, Alaska provided only a miniscule contribution to the national 
total, with fewer than 90,000 acres burned through the end of September. In 
the modern era, national wildfire acreage first topped 9 million in 2006 and 
since then has done so six times (2007, 2012, 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2026), 
with a peak of 10.13 million acres burned in 2015.

Meanwhile, topsoil moisture in the Nation's agricultural regions was rated 
37 percent very short to short on October 4, a significant improvement from 
the 2026 peak of 55 percent on September 6, according to USDA/NASS. However, 
statewide topsoil moisture was still rated at least 70 percent very short to 
short on October 4 in four Western States (Colorado, Nevada, Oregon, and 
Wyoming) and four Mississippi Delta States (Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, 
and Tennessee). Conversely, statewide topsoil moisture on October 4 was rated 
45 percent surplus in Iowa, along with 30 percent in Ohio. The 
U.S. Drought Monitor reflected a balance between worsening drought conditions 
during September in most areas from the southeastern Plains and the western 
Gulf Coast region to the southern Appalachians, and steady or improving 
conditions across large sections of the Plains, West, and upper Midwest. 
Drought was affecting 59.38 percent of the Lower 48 States on September 29, 
marking the 55th consecutive week with national coverage greater than 
40 percent.

On a national scale, crops experiencing notable drought during 2026 included 
cotton, rated 37 percent very poor to poor on October 4, and sorghum, 
34 percent, according to USDA/NASS. Drought impacts were regional for crops 
such as corn and soybeans, with lower crop condition ratings mostly in the 
western Corn Belt. Thirty-eight percent of North Dakota's corn was rated very 
poor to poor on October 4, along with 28 percent of its soybeans, compared to 
respective national values of 18 and 14 percent. However, barely half 
(54 percent) of the Nation's corn was rated good to excellent on October 4, 
along with just 57 percent of the soybeans. Meanwhile, national rangeland and 
pastures rated in very poor to poor condition improved to 49 percent on 
October 4, down from annual peaks of 53 percent on September 6 and 20. In 
some parts of the country, drought-affected rangeland and pastures may not 
fully recover until next spring or beyond, even if favorably wet weather 
prevails, as the window for autumn grass growth will soon close from north to 
south.

Near- or above-normal September temperatures prevailed nationwide, except in 
New England and the Far West. A core area of summer-like heat, with monthly 
temperatures averaging at least 4 to 8 degrees F above normal, stretched from 
central and southern sections of the Rockies and Plains to the southern 
Appalachians, extending as far north as the southern tier of the Corn Belt. 
The late-season heat coincided with a persistent ridge of high pressure that 
remained steadfast across the southern United States for more than 2 months, 
starting in late July. Records for the warmest September on record were set 
in dozens of communities from the south-central United States into the lower 
Midwest, with Abilene, Texas, reporting a monthly average temperature that 
was 9.4 degrees F above normal. The persistent heat (and dryness) favored 
fieldwork and hastened summer crop maturation, but reduced surface water 
supplies and stressed livestock, rangeland, pastures, and immature crops.

September Agricultural Summary

September was warmer than normal for most of the Nation. Parts of the 
Great Plains, lower Missouri Valley, Delta recorded temperatures 6 F or more 
above normal for the month. While much of the Great Basin, Southern Plains, 
and Southeast remained drier than normal, parts of Northwest, Southwest, and 
Northern Plains recorded at least twice the normal amount of precipitation. 
Mainly due to storms connected to El Ni o, parts of the Southwest and western 
Corn Belt recorded 6 inches or more of rain. Some locations in Iowa received 
9 inches or more of rain for the month.

Ninety-six percent of the corn crop was at the dough stage by September 6, 
two percentage points ahead of both last year and the 5-year average.

Statistical Methodology

Field crop survey procedures: Objective yield and farm operator surveys were 
conducted between September 24 and October 5 to gather information on 
expected yield as of October 1. The objective yield surveys for corn, cotton, 
and soybeans were conducted in the major producing States that usually 
account for about 75 percent of the United States production. Randomly 
selected plots were revisited to make current counts. The counts made within 
each sample plot depend on the crop and the maturity of that crop. In all 
cases, plant counts are recorded along with other measurements that provide 
information to forecast the number of ears, bolls, or pods and their weight. 
The counts are used with similar data from previous years to develop a 
projected biological yield. The average harvesting loss is subtracted to 
obtain a net yield. The plots are visited starting in September and are 
revisited each month until crop maturity when the fruit is harvested and 
weighed. After the farm operator has harvested the sample field, another plot 
is sampled to obtain current year harvesting loss. 

The farm operator survey was conducted primarily by telephone with some use 
of mail, internet, and personal interviewers. Approximately 8,300 producers 
were interviewed during the survey period and asked questions about probable 
yield. These growers will continue to be surveyed throughout the growing 
season to provide indications of average yields.

Orange survey procedures: In Florida, during August and September, the number 
of bearing trees and the number of fruit per tree is determined. In August, 
September, December, and March, fruit size measurement and fruit droppage 
surveys are conducted, which are combined with the previous components to 
develop the current forecast of production. California and Texas conduct 
grower surveys on a quarterly basis in October, January, April, and July. 
California also conducts objective measurement surveys in September for Navel 
oranges and in March for Valencia oranges.

Field crop estimating procedures: The Agricultural Statistics Board (ASB) 
reviews multiple data sources at the National and State level, including USDA 
administrative acreage data, satellite imagery, and objective yield and 
grower reported data. All data were reviewed for reasonableness and 
consistency with historical estimates. The data were also reviewed 
considering weather patterns and crop progress compared with previous months 
and previous years.

Orange estimating procedures: State level objective measurement estimates for 
Florida oranges were reviewed for errors, reasonableness, and consistency 
with historical estimates. Reports from growers in California and Texas were 
also used for setting estimates. The ASB analyzes the survey data to prepare 
the published October 1 forecast.

Revision policy: The October 1 production forecast will not be revised; 
instead, a new forecast will be made each month throughout the growing 
season. End-of-season estimates are made after harvest. At the end of the 
marketing season, a balance sheet is calculated using carryover stocks, 
production, exports, millings, feeding, and ending stocks. Revisions are then 
made if the balance sheet relationships or other administrative data warrant 
changes. Estimates of acres for barley, corn, cotton, dry edible beans, oats, 
peanuts, rice, sorghum, soybeans, sugarbeets, and wheat are subject to 
revision in the August Crop Production report. Acres for chickpeas, corn, 
cotton, dry edible peas, lentils, peanuts, rice, sorghum, soybeans, and 
sugarbeets are subject to revision in the September Crop Production report 
each year. Barley, oats, rye, and wheat end-of-season estimates are published 
in the Small Grains Annual Summary report at the end of September. Canola, 
dry edible beans, and sunflower acres are subject to revision in the October 
Crop Production report. Potato acres are subject to revision in the November 
Crop Production report. End-of-season estimates for all other row crops are 
published in the Annual Crop Production Summary in January. Revisions to 
planted acres will only be made when either special survey data, 
administrative data, such as Farm Service Agency program "sign up" data, or 
remote sensing data are available. Harvested acres may be revised any time a 
production forecast is made if there is strong evidence that the intended 
harvested area has changed since the last forecast. End-of-season orange 
estimates will be published in August Citrus Fruits Summary. The orange 
production estimates are based on all data available at the end of the 
marketing season, including information from marketing orders, shipments, and 
processor records. Allowances are made for recorded local utilization and 
home use.

Reliability: To assist users in evaluating the reliability of the October 1 
production forecast, the "Root Mean Square Error," a statistical measure 
based on past performance, is computed. The deviation between the October 1 
production forecast and the final estimate is expressed as a percentage of 
the final estimate. The average of the squared percentage deviations for the 
latest 20-year period is computed. The square root of the average becomes 
statistically the "Root Mean Square Error." Probability statements can be 
made concerning expected differences in the current forecast relative to the 
final end-of-season estimate, assuming that factors affecting this year's 
forecast are not different from those influencing recent years. For example, 
the "Root Mean Square Error" for the October 1 corn for grain production 
forecast is 2.0 percent. This means that chances are 2 out of 3 that the 
current production forecast will not be above or below the final estimate by 
more than 2.0 percent. Chances are 9 out of 10 (90 percent confidence level) 
that the difference will not exceed 3.5 percent.

has been below the final estimate 7 times and above 11 times. This does not

Access to NASS Reports

For your convenience, you may access NASS reports and products the following 
ways:

 	All reports are available electronically, at no cost, on the NASS web 
site: www.nass.usda.gov.

 	The national specific reports are available via a free e-mail 
subscription. To set-up this free subscription, visit www.nass.usda.gov 
and click on "National" in upper right corner above "search" box to 
create an account and select the reports you would like to receive.

 	Economics, Statistics, and Market Information (ESMIS) - National 
Agricultural Library (NAL) website houses NASS's and other agency 
archived reports at https://esmis.nal.usda.gov. All email subscriptions 
containing reports will be sent from https://esmis.nal.usda.gov. To 
receive the reports via e-mail, you will have to go to the website, 
create a new account and subscribe to the reports. You should whitelist 
notifications@esmis.nal.usda.gov in your email client to avoid the 
emails going into spam/junk folders. 

For more information on NASS surveys and reports, call the NASS Agricultural 
Statistics Hotline at (800) 727-9540, 7:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. ET, or e-mail: 
nass@usda.gov. 

If you have specific questions you would like an expert to respond to, please 
visit our "Ask A Specialist" website at 
www.nass.usda.gov/Contact_Us/Ask_a_Specialist.
