A plain-language guide to options on commodity futures. Market numbers in the examples are ICE settlements as of 19 Nov 2026, used only to illustrate; they do not update. Research use only; not investment advice.

An option is a right, not an obligation

A call is the right to buy, and a put is the right to sell, a futures contract at a fixed price (the strike, K) on or before a set day (the expiry). The buyer pays a premium for that right. The seller (the "writer") takes the premium and the obligation.

The whole value of an option comes from one asymmetry: the holder keeps the good outcomes and walks away from the bad ones. If prices could not move, an option would be worth only its intrinsic value.
Buyer (long)Pays the premium. Owns a right. Decides whether to exercise. Most they can lose is the premium.
Seller (short)Receives the premium. Must perform if assigned. Loss on a short call has no ceiling; on a short put it runs to the strike minus the premium.
Exchange (ICE / CME clearing)Stands in the middle. On exercise it assigns a short at random, and both sides get a futures position at the strike.

What is different about options on commodity futures

  • The underlying is a futures contract, not the physical crop. Exercising a March cotton call gives you a long March futures position at the strike. You only get near physical cotton if you then hold that future into delivery.
  • Options expire before the futures' first notice day. Standard options stop trading in the month before the futures month, so the option holder has time to roll or close the futures before deliveries start.
  • Serial and short-dated options. Some option months have no futures month of their own. ICE January cotton options, for example, exercise into March futures. They let you trade a shorter window on the same underlying.
  • American exercise. ICE and CME ag options can be exercised on any business day. Early exercise is rarely worth it: a futures position earns no carry, so exercising only throws away time value. The exception is a deep in-the-money option with almost no time value left, where exercising frees up the interest on the premium.
  • Prices are in the contract's own units. ICE cotton, coffee and sugar trade in US cents per pound (¢/lb); cocoa in $ per tonne; CME grains in ¢ per bushel. So a premium of 1.60 on cotton means 1.60 ¢/lb.

Payoff at expiry vs profit

Call payoff = max(F_T − K, 0) Put payoff = max(K − F_T, 0) Profit = payoff − premium paid

Example: you pay 1.60 ¢/lb for a March 80 cotton call. At expiry March futures are 81.00. You exercise and gain 1.00 ¢/lb, but your profit is 1.00 − 1.60 = −0.60 ¢/lb. Exercise still makes sense, because it gets back part of the premium. The breakeven at expiry is strike + premium = 81.60.

Intrinsic value, time value and moneyness

  • Intrinsic value is what you would get by exercising right now: max(F − K, 0) for a call, max(K − F, 0) for a put.
  • Time value is everything else in the premium. It pays for the chance that prices move your way before expiry, and it melts to zero at expiry.
  • In the money (ITM) means it has intrinsic value. At the money (ATM) means the strike is near the futures price. Out of the money (OTM) means no intrinsic value, only time value.
  • Time value is largest at the money, because that is where the outcome is most uncertain.

Put-call parity on futures

C − P = e^(−rT) × (F − K)

A long call plus a short put at the same strike and expiry behaves exactly like a long future bought at K. So the call and put at one strike must imply the same volatility, and you can build any of the three from the other two:

  • Long call = long put + long futures (a "synthetic call": the classic grower hedge of owning a put against a long position).
  • Long put = long call + short futures.
  • Long futures = long call + short put.

If parity is broken by more than the bid-ask spread, one side is mispriced; in practice the gap is usually stale or wide quotes.

Why uncertainty creates value

Take two futures contracts both at 80 ¢/lb, and an 80-strike call on each.

  • Quiet market: it will almost surely finish near 80. The call is worth almost nothing, because there is no upside to keep.
  • Weather market: it will finish at either 70 or 90, equally likely. The call pays 10 half the time and 0 otherwise, so it is worth about 5 ¢/lb.
Same average price, very different option value. A linear position (futures) only cares about the average outcome. An option has a kinked payoff, so it cares about how wide the spread of outcomes is. Buying an option is buying exposure to the width of the distribution; selling one is selling it.

Two more rules follow. More time can never hurt an American option, because a longer option includes every right of a shorter one. And time and volatility trade places: what matters is total variance, σ²×T. A market with half the daily volatility needs four times as long to give the same option value.

Pricing by replication: a two-state example

March cotton is at 80. Next period it goes to 88 or 72. An 80 call pays 8 or 0. Can we copy that payoff with futures and cash?

Hold h futures: up: h × (88 − 80) = 8 → h = 0.5 down: h × (72 − 80) = −4 (and the call pays 0) The half future gains 4 up and loses 4 down. Add 4 of cash and you get 8 / 0, exactly the call. So the call is worth the 4 of cash (with zero rates).

The hedge ratio of 0.5 is the option's delta. The price came out without anyone's view on whether up or down is more likely. That is the core idea behind every option model: the price is the cost of the hedge, not a forecast.

Chain many short steps together, rehedging at each one, and you get a binomial tree. Make the steps infinitely short and you get Black-Scholes, or for futures, Black-76.

Black-76: the model for options on futures

d1 = [ ln(F/K) + ½σ²T ] / (σ√T) d2 = d1 − σ√T Call = e^(−rT) × [ F·N(d1) − K·N(d2) ] Put = e^(−rT) × [ K·N(−d2) − F·N(−d1) ]

Fischer Black's 1976 version of Black-Scholes uses the futures price F instead of a spot price. A future costs nothing to hold, so there is no carry or dividend term; the only rate is the discount factor e^(−rT) on the premium.

InputEffect on a callEffect on a put
Futures price F upupdown
Strike K updownup
Volatility σ upupup
Time T upup (nearly always)up (nearly always)
Rate r upslightly downslightly down

Handy shortcut: an at-the-money option is worth about 0.4 × F × σ × √T, and a straddle about twice that. March cotton at 79.51, 21.4% vol, 78 days: 0.4 × 79.51 × 0.214 × √(78/365) ≈ 3.1 ¢/lb per option, about 6.3 ¢/lb for the straddle.

Implied volatility (IV)

Every input to Black-76 is visible on a screen except volatility. So traders turn the formula around: take the market premium and solve for the σ that reproduces it. That σ is the implied volatility. There is no closed form, so it is found by trial (Newton's method): guess, reprice, adjust.

IV is a common ruler. Premiums in cents cannot be compared across strikes, expiries or markets; IVs can. A 1.60 ¢ cotton call and a 12 ¢ coffee call mean nothing side by side, but 21% vs 37% does.
  • IV is a price, not a forecast. It is what the market charges for movement. It includes a risk premium (hedgers pay up for protection), supply and demand for options, and model error.
  • Realized volatility (RV) is what the futures actually did, the annualized standard deviation of daily log returns. IV minus RV is the volatility risk premium. It is usually positive.
  • Converting to cents: daily move ≈ F × σ / √252. Expected move to expiry ≈ F × σ × √T.

When IV misleads

  • Options with under 15 days left are left out of IV readings on these desks. Their premiums are tiny and the price curve is flat, so one tick changes the IV a lot, and the last days are dominated by pin effects and event days.
  • Deep in-the-money options are mostly intrinsic value; their IV is unstable for the same reason. Read the smile from out-of-the-money puts on the left and out-of-the-money calls on the right.
  • Wrong forward: serial options (January cotton on March futures) must be priced off the March future, not a nearby.
  • Bid and ask give a range of IVs, not one number. A smile built from mid-prices can show bumps that are only wide quotes.
  • Limit days: when futures lock limit, settlement prices for options can be set off a futures price that did not trade, so that day's IV is not a real market.

Delta: direction

Call Δ = e^(−rT)·N(d1) Put Δ = −e^(−rT)·N(−d1) = Call Δ − e^(−rT)
  • Hedge ratio: a 0.50-delta call moves like half a futures contract. Ten of them = 5 lots of futures exposure. Sell 5 futures and the book is "delta neutral".
  • Slope: delta is the slope of the option-value curve against the futures price.
  • Rough odds: delta is close to, but not the same as, the market-implied chance of finishing in the money. That chance is N(d2), which is a bit lower than a call's N(d1).
  • Call delta minus put delta at the same strike is about 1 (exactly e^(−rT)).

Gamma: how fast the hedge goes stale

Γ = e^(−rT)·φ(d1) / (F·σ·√T) (same for the call and the put)
  • Gamma is the curvature of the option. A long-gamma position gets longer as prices rise and shorter as they fall, so rehedging makes you sell high and buy low.
  • It peaks at the money and grows sharply as expiry approaches (it scales with 1/√T). A short-dated at-the-money option can swing from 0.2 to 0.8 delta on a normal day.
  • Short gamma is the opposite: you are forced to buy rallies and sell breaks, and a big gap hurts the most.

At-the-money March cotton style option, F = K = 80, 21.5% vol, from 180 down to 15 days. Both grow faster the closer expiry gets.

Theta: the rent you pay for gamma

Θ (per year) = −e^(−rT)·F·φ(d1)·σ / (2√T) + r × premium Θ per trading day = Θ / 252
  • A long option loses value every day nothing happens. The seller collects it.
  • Decay is not linear. An at-the-money option loses about half its value in the last quarter of its life, because value scales with √T.
  • Weekends: the model decays over calendar time, but markets only move on trading days. These desks quote theta per trading day so it lines up with daily moves.

Vega: exposure to the vol quote itself

ν = e^(−rT)·F·φ(d1)·√T per 1.00 of vol; divide by 100 for one vol point
  • Vega is the change in premium if IV moves 1 point (say 21% to 22%) with futures unchanged. Hedging with futures does not remove it.
  • It grows with √T, so deferred options (new-crop December cotton, back-month coffee) carry most of a book's vega, while short-dated options carry most of its gamma and theta.
  • Commodity vol jumps around events: WASDE days, Brazil frost scares, US planting weather. Long vega pays when uncertainty is repriced higher even if futures go nowhere.

The one equation that ties them together

Hold an option and hedge its delta with futures. Over one short step, the option's value change is (a Taylor expansion):

Hedged P&L ≈ Θ·δt + ½·Γ·(δF)² + ν·δσ time cost gamma gain vol repricing
A delta-hedged long option loses to time and gains from movement in either direction. A short option is the reverse. Everything below is a consequence of this one line.

Gamma vs theta, and the daily breakeven move

Set the hedged P&L to zero with no vol change: ½Γ(δF)² = −Θ·δt. In Black-76 at a fair price, theta and gamma are locked together:

Θ (per trading day) ≈ −½ · Γ · (F·σ/√252)² Daily breakeven move m = F · σ · √(1/252)
  • If futures move more than m on the day, the long-gamma holder wins; if less, theta wins.
  • m is simply the one-day, one-standard-deviation move the IV is pricing. Cotton at 79.51 with 21.4% vol: 79.51 × 0.214 / 15.87 ≈ 1.07 ¢/lb a day.
  • This is why you hear "the market is paying 107 points a day for March": buying the option is a bet that daily ranges beat that.
  • Squared moves: a day twice the breakeven earns four times the theta; a day at half the breakeven earns only a quarter of it.

Realized vs implied: what a hedged option really bets on

Expected hedged P&L per day ≈ ½ · Γ · F² · (σ_realized² − σ_implied²) / 252
  • Buy at 21% IV, then futures realize 25%: the gamma gains beat the theta paid. Realize 17% and you bleed.
  • The edge is in the squares of the vols, and it is weighted by gamma, so it is earned mostly when price sits near the strike.
  • Path matters. Big moves far from your strike earn little gamma; the same move right at the strike earns a lot.

Skew: why IV differs by strike

Black-76 assumes one vol for every strike. Real markets don't. Plot IV against strike and you get the smile; its tilt is the skew.

  • Equity indices have put skew: crashes are fast and everyone owns stocks, so downside protection is bid.
  • Softs and grains usually have call skew. The big, fast moves are supply shocks up: a frost in Minas Gerais, a drought in West Texas, a short-crop squeeze. Consumers (mills, roasters) and short hedgers who sold forward buy upside calls; growers sell them against their crop. So OTM calls trade over OTM puts.
  • Skew changes with the price level. After a long fall, put skew can appear as specs pile into downside; after a squeeze, call skew steepens.

25-delta risk reversal (RR25) = IV(25Δ call) − IV(25Δ put) positive = calls over puts 25-delta butterfly (BF25) = ½[IV(25Δ call) + IV(25Δ put)] − IV(ATM) = how curved the smile is

Term structure: IV by expiry

Cotton ATM IV by option expiry, 19 Nov 2026
  • Commodity term structures follow the crop calendar. Cotton old-crop options (March to July) price 21–23% while new-crop September to December 2027 price about 18%: the old crop has a known, tight story; the new crop is a year away and gets averaged out.
  • Vol tends to rise into the growing season for the crop at risk (US cotton in July to August, Brazil coffee in June to August frost months, US corn in June to July).
  • A scheduled event only lifts options that expire after it.

Forward vol: the vol between two expiries

Variance adds over time. Total variance to an expiry is w = σ² × T. The vol priced for the window between two expiries is:

σ(1→2) = √[ (σ2²·T2 − σ1²·T1) / (T2 − T1) ]

For cotton the March and May options sit on different futures, so this is only an approximation; it is exact for two expiries on the same future (for example January and March options, both on March futures). A later expiry can have lower IV than an earlier one without any arbitrage, as long as total variance still rises.

Producer (grower, sugar mill, coffee farmer): protect the floor

A grower expects to sell 250,000 lb of cotton (5 contracts) in the spring and is worried prices fall. Three choices:

  • Sell futures: locks the price; no upside if a squeeze comes, and margin calls if prices rally.
  • Buy puts: a floor at strike minus premium, every cent of upside kept. Premium is paid up front.
  • Fence (buy a put, sell a call): a floor and a ceiling for much less premium. In call-skewed softs the call sold is relatively rich, which helps the producer.

All are futures-price hedges. The physical price is futures plus basis, which these options do not cover.

Consumer (textile mill, roaster, refiner): cap the cost

  • The risk is a rally. A mill buying cotton on-call has its basis set but still has to fix the futures price later. If it waits and futures squeeze, its yarn margin disappears.
  • Buy calls to cap the fixing price, or a call spread (buy 82, sell 90) when it only fears a moderate rise and wants to spend less. Call skew makes outright calls dear, so spreads are common.
  • Long fence (buy a call, sell a put) gives a cap and a floor, so the mill gives up some benefit of a fall.

Merchant: hedge the book, not just the trade

  • A merchant's flat-price risk on physical is normally hedged with futures; what it really trades is basis and spreads. Options come in when it offers clients price protection.
  • Offer a grower a "minimum price" contract and the merchant is short a put to the grower; it buys the matching put on ICE or carries the exposure and delta-hedges it.
  • Offer a mill a "maximum price" contract and the merchant is short a call; same choice.
  • A book of client options is then run on its net greeks: delta hedged daily with futures, with gamma and vega limits.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurts
Reading IV from options with under 15 days leftTiny premiums and flat price curves make the IV jump on single ticks. These desks leave them out of IV readings.
Treating delta as the probability of profitDelta is a hedge ratio. The chance of finishing ITM is N(d2), and the chance of profit is lower still, because you need to clear the premium too.
Selling options as "income"You are paid theta for being short gamma. Most days you win small; a weather shock or squeeze takes it back with interest.
Ignoring limit movesCotton, coffee and sugar futures have daily price limits or circuit breakers. On a locked day you cannot delta hedge, and options may become the only thing that trades.
Forgetting what you get on exerciseExercise or assignment gives a futures position the next morning, with margin. Short options left open into expiry can leave an unwanted futures position (pin risk), just before first notice.
Pricing serials off the wrong futureJanuary cotton options exercise into March futures; pricing them off the old December contract gives nonsense vols.
Using one vol for every strikeThe smile matters. A 90 call priced at the ATM vol will look cheap when it isn't.
Mixing calendar and trading daysPick one: here T uses calendar days/365 and daily moves, theta and breakeven use 252 trading days.
Forgetting vega in deferred monthsA delta-hedged December option can lose more on a 2-point IV drop than on a week of theta.
Comparing spreads with mid-pricesThin strikes have wide markets; a strategy that looks free at mid can cost a tick or two per leg to trade.
Assuming a hedge covers basisFutures options hedge the futures price only. A grower with a put still bears basis risk.
